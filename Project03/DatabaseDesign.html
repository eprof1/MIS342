Project03 Database Structure Review Guide

Project03 starts with instructor-provided tables and relationships. Your job is not to redesign the entire database from scratch. Inspect the structure, identify and correct material problems when necessary, and then build the application layer.

1. Confirm the business purpose

  • What business processes do the supplied tables represent?
  • What information must employees enter, retrieve, update, summarize, and report?
  • What decisions should the application support?

2. Inspect each table

  • Does the table represent one main subject?
  • Is there a primary key that uniquely identifies each record?
  • Are field names understandable and data types appropriate?
  • Are required fields, defaults, validation rules, and indexes appropriate for the business rule?
  • Are there repeated groups or obvious duplicated facts that should be stored elsewhere?

3. Inspect relationships and referential integrity

  • Identify every primary-key/foreign-key relationship used by the application.
  • Verify one-to-many, one-to-one, and junction-table relationships where present.
  • Confirm that related key fields use compatible data types.
  • Evaluate whether referential integrity should be enforced and whether cascade options are appropriate.

4. Evaluate normalization through at least 3NF

Use normalization as a diagnostic tool, not as a requirement to maximize the number of tables.

  • 1NF: fields contain atomic values and there are no repeating groups.
  • 2NF: non-key facts depend on the whole key.
  • 3NF: non-key facts depend on the key rather than on other non-key facts.

If you identify a possible normalization issue, explain the business consequence before changing the supplied structure.

5. Plan the application objects from business tasks

Do not create objects merely to satisfy a count. Every object must have a stated purpose.

  • Queries: what information question or data-change task does each query support?
  • Forms: who uses the form, for what workflow, and what input/validation controls are needed?
  • Reports: who is the audience, what decision does the report support, and what grouping/summary is useful?
  • Macros/events/VBA: what repeated workflow should be automated, and what happens if the automation is wrong?

6. Test before declaring an object complete

For important objects, use a small test plan containing:

  • normal valid case;
  • boundary or unusual valid case;
  • invalid/missing input;
  • no-match or empty-result case when relevant;
  • multiple-match/duplicate case when relevant;
  • known expected calculation or total.

7. Verify the complete application

  • Can a new user navigate the application without depending on the Access Navigation Pane?
  • Do forms update the intended records?
  • Do queries return correct rows/calculations?
  • Do reports agree with their source queries?
  • Do macros/VBA perform the intended business rule?
  • Can the application recover from an error or bad input without damaging the data?

8. Use AI as a reviewer, not as proof

Hermes or the course AI assistant may explain, critique, propose SQL/VBA, generate test cases, and help troubleshoot. A generated answer is not evidence. Test every accepted recommendation in Access and preserve human approval for data-changing or automated actions.
